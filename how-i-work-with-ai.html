01 / AI viewpoint · Research

Is AI Different This Time?

A Mixed-Methods Study of AI Adoption in China
My research asked how AI is changing early-career work — and what that means for how juniors learn, develop judgment, and grow into larger responsibility.

Read the paper on Overleaf ↗
What I took from the research

The safest direction is not a permanently “safe” occupation, but work that keeps me close to real-world feedback, progressive responsibility, and opportunities to redesign how humans and AI work together.

What I found
1. AI changes tasks before whole jobs.

Entry-level cognitive tasks shift first. Human value therefore moves away from execution alone and toward verification, problem framing, contextual judgment, task decomposition, coordination, ownership, and deciding how AI should fit into the wider workflow.

2. The Junior Paradox

AI can make juniors productive faster, while weakening some of the repeated real-world exposure through which tacit knowledge, pattern recognition, and professional judgment used to develop.

3. The answer is AI-mediated apprenticeship + progressive ownership.

What needs to be preserved is not inefficient work itself, but the learning process it used to create: verification, explicit reasoning, senior feedback, lower-risk decisions, and responsibility for progressively larger outcomes.

02.1 / AI practice · At work

How I use AI at work.

I decided to test the research on myself. Instead of using AI only to finish tasks faster, I use it to make the surrounding work easier to understand.

AI does the first pass. I use the review to reconstruct the workstream, decision logic, dependencies, and what could come next.
The paper became a learning loop. I now use that loop on real projects to move from completing a task toward understanding — and eventually owning — a larger piece of the work.
AI still cannot replace the apprenticeship around the work. I still need feedback, mentoring, lower-risk decisions, and real opportunities to take responsibility.

AI-mediated Apprenticeship · in practice

AI as a learning coach, not just a summarizer.

I built the learning loop that my research suggested was becoming necessary: use AI for the first pass, then make the reasoning, decisions, feedback, and ownership visible enough to learn from.

“What is the real workstream, and what outcome is it trying to produce?”
“What decision is being made, who has the decision right, and what information or trade-offs shaped it?”
“What dependency or blocker is emerging — and what happened after the decision?”
“What did a senior colleague notice that I missed, what can I prepare before being asked, and what defined piece could I own next?”
Progressive Ownership:
Execution → Verification → Bounded Decision-Making → Ownership → Workflow / System Redesign

Moving from someone who completes assigned tasks toward someone who understands and takes responsibility for a larger outcome.
01

From meeting notes → decision learning

I reconstruct the trigger, options, trade-offs, decision right, evidence used, and what happened after the decision — not only the summary and action list.

Output → understanding how the organization decides.
02

From “update this” → map the workstream

I ask what outcome the task serves, what it depends on, who owns the surrounding process, and which blocker or requirement is actually shaping progress.

Task → context → dependency → workstream.
03

From follow-up → proactive preparation

I use the wider context to anticipate the next information need, trade-off, dependency, or stakeholder question — and prepare before being asked.

Reaction → prediction → lower-risk judgment.
04

From helping → progressive ownership

I look for a bounded piece where I can take responsibility for the outcome, while being explicit about the feedback, authority, and decision support I still need.

“Can I help?” → “Can I own this defined piece?”

My approach

Small friction → working prototype.

I build when a workflow feels unnecessarily heavy, when an idea keeps coming back, or when I want a tool that does not quite exist in the form I need.

AI makes experimentation cheap enough to be part of everyday life.

What changed for me

AI changed both sides of my work: how I learn inside organizations, and what I feel capable of creating outside them. I still use it to save time — but I care much more about what I do with the time, attention, and capability it gives back.
